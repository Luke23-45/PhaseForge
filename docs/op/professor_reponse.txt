## 2. What Agarwal et al. (2021) actually supports — and the gap in how it's applied here

I read the full paper summary directly. Two things matter for your D1 decision:

**What it actually says about run counts:** 3-10 runs per task are prevalent in deep RL because evaluating more is often computationally prohibitive — that's a statement about what's *typical*, not what's *sufficient*. The paper's own case study is more pessimistic: while folk wisdom suggests 20-30 runs are enough, the authors find the number needed to reliably estimate a median score is closer to 50-100 runs on Atari 100k, and even 100 runs weren't enough to reliably detect a "true" improvement of 0% lift (i.e., distinguishing noise from itself) — statistically defensible improvement detection only occurred at 25-100 runs depending on effect size. So "5-10 seeds needed to claim differences" understates how conservative the paper actually is.

**The bigger issue: their statistical efficiency comes from aggregating across many tasks, not from a clever CI formula.** The stratified bootstrap and IQM machinery gets its power from pooling M tasks × N runs; bootstrapping over a single task with small N is explicitly called out as less reliable, which is why they aggregate across tasks instead. PhaseForge's Lift evaluation is **M = 1 task**. Applying the "Agarwal toolkit" here doesn't get you the sample-efficiency benefit the paper is famous for — you're in the exact degenerate case (M=1, N=3) their method is weakest at.

This actually validates something the report already does well: it correctly separates **per-seed Wilson CIs** (episode-sampling uncertainty for a fixed trained policy) from **across-seed spread** (training uncertainty). That's the right conceptual split and a lot of papers get this wrong.

Two concrete upgrades I'd make to the stats section:

1. **The pooled-150-episode Wilson CI understates uncertainty.** Pooling all 150 episodes across 3 seeds as if i.i.d. ignores that episodes from the same seed are correlated (same trained policy) — this is pseudoreplication. A seed-stratified bootstrap (resample seeds with replacement, mirroring Agarwal's per-task stratification but applied to seeds) would be more honest, though with only 3 seeds its resolution will still be coarse — worth reporting anyway, since a wider-but-honest interval is better than a narrower-but-wrong one.
2. **Compute probability-of-improvement, not just CI-overlap.** Agarwal et al. explicitly recommend average probability of improvement — P(X>Y) — as more informative than checking whether interval estimates overlap, since overlapping CIs is a conservative and sometimes misleading heuristic for "no difference". With your per-episode Bernoulli outcomes this is a cheap bootstrap computation (e.g., "72% probability PhaseForge beats Warm-Start MoE") and will likely be more informative than "all intervals overlap."

Also worth citing alongside Agarwal: Colas, Sigaud & Oudeyer (2018), "How Many Random Seeds? Statistical Power Analysis in Deep Reinforcement Learning Experiments," gives explicit t-test and bootstrap-based formulas for computing how many seeds you need to detect a given effect size — this is the right tool for a data-driven answer to D1(b) rather than picking "3 more" arbitrarily. Henderson et al. 2018 ("Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters," AAAI 2018) is the standard companion citation on seed sensitivity being a systemic RL/continuous-control problem, not something specific to your method.

## 3. The auxiliary-task-conflict literature — which fix actually matches your failure mode

Your diagnosis (phase head overfits, val/loss_phase rises from ~0.7 to ~2.5-2.7 while train accuracy hits 0.96) is a real, well-documented phenomenon, and your controls (Phase-Pretrain spread 0.24→0.08 with random router; Plain-Encoder spread 0.04 with a stable encoder) are genuinely convincing — that's a clean 2-cell isolation of the interaction effect. Good work.

But I'd push back gently on reaching straight for PCGrad/CAGrad/ForkMerge. Those methods solve a *different* symptom than the one you've documented:

- PCGrad and CAGrad are built around gradients from different tasks actively conflicting — pointing away from each other in a way that hurts the *main* task's optimization. In your case, val/loss_action isn't degrading — it's flat across the plateau. The main task is fine. What's varying is which of many equally-good-for-action encoder solutions gets picked, and how much phase-relevant structure that particular solution happens to preserve. That's a different mechanism: a **flat-plateau selection problem**, not an active-conflict problem.
- GradNorm balances gradient *magnitudes* across tasks so no task dominates training dynamics — also a good but slightly orthogonal fix, since your problem isn't that phase-loss gradients are too large or small, it's that the auxiliary signal stops generalizing partway through training.
- Du et al. 2018's adaptive gradient-similarity weighting is specifically designed to detect the moment an auxiliary loss stops helping and downweight it dynamically — this is the most mechanistically on-target of the four, since it's built exactly for "when does my auxiliary task start hurting" rather than "how do I resolve simultaneous conflicting improvement directions."

Given that, here's what I'd actually try, cheapest first (stage-1 is CPU-only, ~6 min/seed per your own numbers):

1. **Checkpoint tie-break (near-zero cost):** within the ~2%-band val/loss_action plateau, break ties using minimum val/loss_phase instead of picking arbitrarily. If your per-epoch checkpoints from the corrected run are still on disk, this costs nothing — you can re-select from existing checkpoints without retraining. This is the most direct fix for the exact bug you found (an underspecified selection rule on a flat plateau), and it stays faithful to "select on val/loss_action" — it's a documented tie-break, not a change of primary metric.
2. **If (1) isn't enough, try a decaying λ_phase or Du-et-al.-style adaptive λ(t).** This targets the actual failure (phase supervision helps early, then overfits) more precisely than fixed λ=1.0 for all 200 epochs.
3. **Only escalate to PCGrad/CAGrad/ForkMerge if (1) and (2) fail and you've confirmed real gradient conflict** — which you can check almost for free by logging cos(∇L_action, ∇L_phase) per step (exactly Du et al.'s / PCGrad's diagnostic). This tells you empirically whether "auxiliary conflict" or "flat-plateau lottery" is the dominant mechanism, rather than inferring it only from the loss-curve shape.

One more cheap, complementary diagnostic: since your rule-based labeler uses median filtering and minimum-duration thresholds, part of the phase-head's validation degradation could be **boundary label noise** rather than pure interference — i.e., errors clustering near phase transitions rather than being uniform. You already have per-epoch validation predictions logged; a breakdown of phase-head error rate by distance-to-transition would distinguish "overfitting to demo-specific idiosyncrasies" from "the labeler's decision boundary is inherently noisy" — these call for different fixes (label smoothing / boundary tolerance vs. λ scheduling).

Whatever fix you pick, document it with the same rigor as the loss_total→loss_action fix (new commit, before/after determinism check) — that's what makes the report credible right now, and a silent post-hoc protocol change would undercut it.

## 4. One naming correction: your router isn't "Switch-Transformer-style"

Switch Transformer's defining, named contribution is top-1 routing — replacing the standard top-k approach with k=1, specifically to simplify away from k>1. Top-2 gating with noisy training-time routing and a load-balancing loss is the original Shazeer et al. (2017) "Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts" design, later scaled up in GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020) which also uses top-2. Your architecture (top-2-of-6, noisy gate, load-balancing loss) is Shazeer/GShard-style, not Switch-Transformer-style. This is a small fix but worth making before this goes in front of anyone who works on MoE — "Switch Transformer" specifically signals top-1 to that audience, and it's an easy thing for a reviewer to flag.
